The first thing she bought was a lamp.

Not a desk, not a chair, not the shelves she had been saving pictures of for months. A lamp with a pleated shade and a small brass switch. It made the room feel as though someone had been expecting her.

For years, she had borrowed corners. The end of a kitchen table, a chair beside a sleeping child, a narrow patch of sofa after everyone else had gone to bed. She had learned to make herself small without noticing.

The room was not large. The desk was second-hand, the floorboards complained, and the window looked onto a brick wall. But the key in her pocket belonged to her.

Every morning she opened the curtains, made tea, and sat down for twenty minutes. Sometimes she wrote. Sometimes she read. Sometimes she watched the dust move through the light.

The ritual was modest, almost invisible. But it changed the shape of the day.

A room, she discovered, was not simply a place to put things. It was a place where a person could begin to hear herself again.
