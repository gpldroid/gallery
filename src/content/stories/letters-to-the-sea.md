The house had been closed for eleven months, but the sea had kept visiting.

It left salt on the windows, a fine white line along the garden gate, and the familiar sound of water moving against stone. Lena found the letters in a biscuit tin beneath the stairs.

There were seventeen of them. Each addressed to her mother in the same careful handwriting, each sealed, each dated on a Sunday.

She sat on the floor and opened the first.

It began with an apology. Not the kind that asks to be forgiven, but the kind that finally tells the truth. The writer spoke of a summer long before Lena was born, of a promise made near the harbour, of a train that had left before anyone could change their mind.

By the fifth letter, the afternoon had turned gold.

Lena carried the tin outside. The tide was low, and the rocks shone like dark glass. She read the last letter aloud, though no one was there to hear it.

When she finished, she folded the paper along its old creases and placed it back in the tin.

Some things, she understood, were not meant to be answered. They were meant to be found.
